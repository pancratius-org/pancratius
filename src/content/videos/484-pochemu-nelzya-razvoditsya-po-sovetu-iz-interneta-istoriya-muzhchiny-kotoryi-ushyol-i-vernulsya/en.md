Why, in a relationship crisis, do we trust strangers more than our own family? He had been married to his wife for twenty years, left home under the influence of online videos, and one night wondered who he was learning about family life from.

Relationship psychology is simple: the more intense the conflicts in the family, the more you want to trust someone who promises an easy solution. Video after video explained to him that his wife was unfit, that he shouldn’t tolerate her, that leaving was the right thing to do. He believed her.

And then, that night, in an empty apartment, he asked himself a question that chilled him: who am I learning about family life from right now? This is how the most subtle mistakes in relationships begin—those that are born not in an argument, but in someone else’s text.

This real-life story is a short, meaningful tale about how strangers’ voices can replace the memory of the person with whom you’ve spent half your life. We’re used to looking for family psychology in other people’s advice, and relationship problems in videos where everything is laid out point by point. But family stories and life stories are rarely resolved from the outside.

The psychology of relationships between a man and a woman begins where other people’s opinions end: in conversation, in silence, in the ability to see a specific person next to you. There are no guilty parties and no easy answers.

The question is: who really decides for you whom you love? And how many years of your life are you willing to sacrifice for someone else’s opinion of your marriage?
